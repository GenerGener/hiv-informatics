## Abstract

Advances in the study of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) track with advances in nucleic acid sequencing. However, there remains no unified framework for approaching the types of sequences seen across the HIV replication cycle, preventing comprehensive studies in more complex HIV experimental systems. Overly simplified models of HIV proviral and virion genomes, ignorance of HIV splice isoforms, and inconsistent reagent verification prevent the observation of HIV heterogeneity by way of sequence variability, obscuring biological insights. **The establishment of HIV Informatics as a framework for approaching HIV sequence variability across HIV’s diverse forms enables investigators and clinicians to maximize the information gained from their assays.** In this dissertation, I:

- Established HIV Informatics as a framework unifying HIV models, nucleic acid (re)sequencing, bioinformatics, and validation to maximize the HIV information gained from assays

- Evaluated modern sequencing methods for recovering HIV-1 information from key reagents/models

- Found *in vitro* HIV-1 transductions are more like transfections with HIV-1 virions delivering packaged HIV-1 transcripts from 293T producer cells to CD4+ T cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells.

- Defined comprehensive HIV transcript models from long- and short-read data, enabling HIV-1 gene expression studies

- Identified up to 15 new putative HIV-1 proteins conserved across HIV-1 and simian immunodeficiency viruses 

- Leveraged insights from above to follow HIV-1 sequence variability during acute HIV-1 encephalopathy in HIV patients

This dissertation is divided into three parts. **“Part One: HIV, HIV (re)sequencing, and a new framework to maximize HIV information from samples”** serves as an introduction, divided into four chapters. **Chapter 1** describes select information about what is known about HIV/AIDS and focuses our discussion on HIV. **Chapter 2** describes select methods used to elucidate HIV information (HIV informatics, with a little “i”) in experimental and clinical settings. **Chapter 3** introduces HIV Informatics (big “I”) as a framework unifying HIV models, nucleic acid (re)sequencing, bioinformatics, and validation to maximize the HIV information gained from assays. **Chapter 4** discusses the problems that will be addressed in the subsequent chapters. I begin the discussion of my experimental findings in **“Part Two: Maximizing HIV sequencing information from control samples and model systems”**. This part includes **Chapter 5**, where I discuss advances in HIV DNA space. We then transition into the RNA space, where we discuss advances such as HIV mRNA models (**Chapter 6)** and HIV virion mRNA (**Chapter 7**). This part concludes by analyzing HIV RNA with LTR phasing in **Chapter 8**, with the paradigm-shifting finding that *in vitro* HIV-1 transductions are more like transfections with HIV-1 virions delivering packaged HIV-1 transcripts from 293T producer cells to CD4+ T cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells. **“Part Three: Maximizing HIV sequence information from heterogeneous samples”** includes a discussion about interrogating HIV in non-model systems in **Chapter 9**, specifically recovering HIV information from post-mortem HIV encephalopathy patients with single-nuclear RNA sequencing. We were able to follow limited HIV evolution in noisy samples, as well as detect the new isoforms seen in Chapter 6, illustrating the benefit of HIV Informatics to enable the study of HIV across the spectrum of HIV samples just short of clinical samples. This dissertation concludes in **Chapter 10** with a discussion and future directions, including a call to action for third-party validation of findings presented herein.
