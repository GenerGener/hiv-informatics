# Chapter 4: Problems and Challenges addressed by HIV Informatics

**Chapter Abstract**

Chapter 4 explicitly introduces the problems I addressed with HIV Informatics by my papers and/or meeting abstracts included in my dissertation.

## Introduction

Identifying AIDS as a clinical syndrome in 1981 was seminal. Identifying HIV as the cause of AIDS in 1983 was the next key event in the HIV/AIDS epidemic, now an established pandemic. A third key event was the completion of the first HIV-1 reference genome in 1985 (Ratner et al. 1985). This watershed event enabled screening and later diagnostic tests, molecular dissection of the virus, and improved drug development.

HIV/AIDS was not just an immunodeficiency. Many tissues could become infected by HIV, and patients presented heterogeneously. For example, elite controllers rarely got sick despite being demonstrably HIV-positive, or if they did, their immune decline took on a much more indolent course. As more HIV sequences were collected from patients, investigators began to better appreciate HIV sequence heterogeneity. This heterogeneity became apparent as viral evolution occurred in earlier treatment regimens with fewer, often more toxic, drugs were used prior to the advent of multi-drug regimens. Investigators began to try to model heterogeneous sequences within patients as quasispecies, focusing on clinically relevant segments of the *pol* gene: reverse transcriptase, protease, and later integrase. However, technological limitations prevented such HIV information from making it into patient diagnostics and monitoring until very recently. Instead, combination therapy was developed empirically, one clinical trial at a time. Today we rely on viral load as a clinical endpoint instead of capturing HIV sequence information directly. Exceptions are viral genotyping which is performed in resource-rich settings either before antiretroviral therapy initiation and/or in the event of a need for treatment interruption or reinitiation. Independently of HIV genotyping, molecular epidemiologists sequenced *env* genes to track the spread of HIV. All of this was done as new methods became available, and over many years. Since sequencing was not available earlier in the HIV/AIDS pandemic, HIV heterogeneity was never assessed as a contributing factor for differential progression of HIV. However, some evidence supports differences in infecting virus and differences in clinical outcome. For example, it is well known that patients infected with HIV-2, a distantly related virus believed to be caused by the entry of another primate immunodeficiency virus into the human population, usually causes a much less severe AIDS-like illness.

People living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) are living longer (Alejandro R Gener 2021), and to maintain this progress it remains important to determine which tissues can serve as reservoirs for HIV replication. Beyond preventing HIV replication, HIV treatment does not prevent HIV mRNA transcription or translation into HIV proteins (together: HIV gene expression). This is because in the context of viral suppression, HIV gene expression continues in these reservoir cells populations. It remains important to be able to recover HIV sequence information and to distinguish between HIV’s myriad nucleic acid forms (Gener and Kimata 2019) to be able to track HIV across the human body.

We now have the tools to address HIV heterogeneity in clinical samples. However, it serves to reason that it is beneficial to revisit defined key reagents before exploring HIV in heterogeneous clinical samples. HIV Informatics is a framework unifying HIV models, nucleic acid (re)sequencing, bioinformatics, and validation to maximize the HIV information gained from assays.

In Chapter 5 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021a), 6 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021b), 7 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021c), and 8 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021d) I revisited basic historical models of HIV-1 previously taken for granted with new methods. Reference reagents like HIV-1 plasmids or virus preparations (preps) are rarely routinely sequence (re)verified/(re)validated. That “re” is important to prevent a game of biological telephone from propagating into costly experimental error. Until recently, repetitive sequences in the 5’ and 3’ LTRs were ignored because of cost to validate them between *in silico* reference genomes and physical copies available through the AIDs Reagent Program (ARP; and more recently Addgene) or across individual labs. There is no standard annotation for HIV reference genomes. Annotation is a problem with HIV sequences. For example, the ones in Chapter 7 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021c) Table 1 lack *gag-pol*, and many complete HIV and HIV-related genomes lack feature annotations altogether. Many papers and textbooks fail to even include the *gag-pol* gene model, instead favoring out-of-date *gag* and *pol* as distinct. This may be a holdover from the study of endogenous retroviruses, or simple retroviruses[^1]. That said, HIV is far from a simple retrovirus, and including molecular biologically relevant genes may facilitate better understanding.

### Validating HIV-1 reference genome(s)

Chapter 5 dealt with comparing new nanopore long-read DNA sequencing to validate pHXB2_D, the only known physical copy of *the* canonical HIV-1 reference genome strain HXB2 (*in silico* as GenBank:K03455.1 and RefSeq:NC_001802.1) which is currently not available in the ARP. A recently rederived plasmid based on pNL4-3 backbone was included as a positive control. Variants were noted in pHXB2_D. This was surprising because physical key reagents like molecular clones are assumed to be identical to their *in silico* genome accessions. The repetitive elements in both plasmids’ HIV-1 LTRs were phased and validated with 1.) current gold-standard short-read (SR) 150 base-long Illumina sequencing and assembly and 2.) new long-read (LR) ~2000 base-long Nanopore DNA sequencing, assembly, and polishing. The bioinformatics applied depended on which methods were used. For example, SR aligners can usually only take SR data as input. An exception is Minimap2, which recently implemented SR alignment (Heng Li, personal communication). However, the internal algorithms used depend on data type. As such, aligners are often not interchangeable. Downstream analyses may be flexible. An exception is transcript modelers, which rely on accuracy of mapped reads to make transcript models and to perform transcript profiling. Transcript profiling estimates transcript abundance based on premade (user-provided) or *de novo* transcript models. *De novo* in this context means transcript models generated from a given sample only, without prior feature (gene or transcript) information. Variants were called manually based on K03455.1, and as such this was a resequencing experiment. LANL HIV Sequence Database’s HXB2 K03455.1 is modeled as a provirus with likely incorrectly phased long-terminal repeats (my first paper) (Gener et al. 2021). As this was the first time LR and SR data was used to evaluate an HXB2 sequence, this pHXB2_D assembly is currently the highest quality HXB2 assembly to-date.

### Redressing overly simplified HIV-1 mRNA models

Previously, HIV-1 mRNA was modeled roughly as 3 different classes of mRNAs: unspliced 9kb, singly spliced 4 kb, and doubly spliced ~2 kb mRNAs (Purcell and Martin 1993). The HIV research community took Purcell and Martin’s results and ran with them, focusing on the most common isoforms to design PCR primers for reverse transcription (RT) PCR with a gel band readout. This was subsequently adapted to real-time RT qPCR, which is currently a staple in many labs. The HIV research community also ran with the assumption that the first open reading frame (ORF) was the sole or predominant protein product in each isoform. Singly vs. doubly spliced classifications ignore the heterogeneity revealed by Schwartz et al., Purcell and Martin, and others. Newcomers Ocwieja et al. in 2012 and Nguyen Quang et al. in 2020 attempted to capture HIV splice junctions comprehensively but fell short of providing usable models and tractable bioinformatic pipelines which accommodate high-throughput and/or next-generation sequencing data. Their work has not yet been adopted widely, in part because their tables of splice junctions cannot be easily integrated into public database which accept formats like FASTA, GenBank, Gene transfer format (.gtf), etc.

HIV-1 review resources report discrepancies between earlier primary reports. For example, the ViralZone “HIV replication cycle” review says the following: “\[HIV-1\] mRNA transcripts are alternatively spliced into six mRNAs.” However, this review does not support this claim with specific citations, but I can deduce based on what I have seen so far in my readings. One isoform (#1) is unspliced HIV. That is believed to make Gag and Gag-pol. Another (#2) is *gag-pol* spliced out, yielding Vif. Another (#3) is *gag-pol* and *vif* spliced out, yielding Vpr. Another (#4) is *gag-pol*, *vif*, and *vpr* spliced out, yielding Vpu and Env. Another (#5) is *gag-pol*, *vif*, *vpr*, *vpu*, and *env* spliced out, yielding Tat and Rev. Last (#6), maximally spliced HIV can yield Nef only. Other reviews cite different numbers of isoforms (UCSF HIV InSite claims 30 (Schwartz et al. 1990)), and often stop short of linking isoforms with the open reading frames and therefore their HIV protein-coding capacity.

Chapter 6 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021b) defined models of HIV-1 mRNA explicitly, which had not been defined comprehensively before. My work in Chapter 6 offers others the chance to build their own models from their experiments if their data are of sufficient coverage and high quality (read length and read quality). My methods are backwards-compatible with SR RNA-seq. However, LR data is better for capturing splice junctions present in HIV-1 mRNA.

### A new HIV-1 virion model

Chapter 7 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021c) explored HIV virion mRNA directly as native RNA for the first time. While Nguyen Quang et al. in 2020 produced the highest-quality LR dataset from infected cell lysates, they were unable to produce HIV virion mRNA. We sequenced virion mRNA directly without cDNA sequencing and compared our results with the transcript models we built in Chapter 6 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021b). This was an important example of cross-validation using datasets from multiple labs and some open datasets. Lack of open datasets had limited progress in this space previously. My HIV virion mRNA results challenge the dogmatic assumption that HIV mRNA includes two full-length unspliced HIV mRNA transcripts of approximately 9.7 kb. I proposed the Hybrid Segmented Retrovirus model (Gener and Kimata 2020), of which HIV is the index example.

Chapter 8 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021d) revisited LR HIV mRNA datasets from Chapters 6 and 7 with LTR phasing from Chapter 5, with surprising results which challenge the current model of retroviral reverse transcription. Careful inspection of R variants across NL4-3 in Nguyen Quang et al. 2020’s and my native virion datasets show that LTRs do not homogenize as expected. This supports the model of HIV virion as a bag of heterogeneous HIV mRNAs as opposed to having two copies of full-length unspliced HIV mRNAs.

In Chapter 9 (Alejandro R. Gener 2021e) I built on lessons learned from implementing HIV Informatics in control systems as I attempt to maximize the information extracted from HIV-infected cadaveric brain tissue to clarify the contributions of different cell populations toward HIV gene expression during HIV encephalopathy.
